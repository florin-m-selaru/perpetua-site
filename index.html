The problem

Patients lose track of their own disease between visits.

IBD has no cure and no straight line. Symptoms come and go over weeks, medications change, and there are colonoscopies, lab tests, urgent-care visits, and the occasional course of steroids. Then, at a fifteen-minute appointment, the patient has to reconstruct all of it from memory and decide what mattered most. The patient also has to make clinically relevant associations, such as between symptoms and the type of food eaten, activities, or the timing and doses of medications.

Daily symptom diaries were meant to solve this, but they rarely get used. Filling out a form at the clinic desk does not work well. There is not enough room to write everything down and not enough time to do it, and no one remembers every detail, especially when they are anxious or unwell, which is usually the reason for the visit. In an early survey of people with IBD, most said that forgetting details before an appointment was a leading frustration, and most relied on memory or a notes app between visits.

When fewer details are lost between visits, patients understand their illness better, and clinicians can help them make better decisions.

How it works

One record, one story.

Capture

Record a voice note whenever something happens, whether it is a bad night, a missed dose, a new medication, or a trip to urgent care. Perpetua asks one or two short follow-up questions in plain language, and it never asks you to rate anything from one to ten. On the days when nothing changes, there is nothing to fill in.

Your story

Your entries build into a timeline that places your symptoms next to treatment changes and clinical events, compared with your own baseline and past flares. Seen together, they make patterns easier to recognize: a symptom that tracks with a food, an activity, or a change in medication.

The visit

Before an appointment, Perpetua drafts a short summary for your clinician: what changed, when it changed, how it compares with your usual, and which treatments or events matter for reading it. You review the summary and share it yourself.

What Perpetua is not

Perpetua listens, clarifies, and organizes. It never gives medical advice.

  • It does not diagnose, estimate disease activity, or tell you whether you are in a flare.
  • It does not advise you to start, stop, or change any medication.
  • It does not contact your clinic or send anything for you. You share your summary yourself.
  • It does not replace your care. If something is urgent, it tells you to call your clinic, or 911.
  • Your entries stay on your phone. Nothing about you is stored in the cloud.
  • It does not invent numbers you did not give it.

The thesis

The record grows more valuable the longer it is kept.

Symptoms only mean something in relation to the individual: a person's baseline, their earlier flares, and the treatments they were taking when things changed. A record built that way becomes more useful every month it exists, first to the patient, then to the clinician, and in time to research. Perpetua is built in that order, and each stage has to earn the next.

Stage 1The prototypenow

An iPhone app for adults with established Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis, built with Modern Logic and now being evaluated with patients at Johns Hopkins. It organizes the patient's own record so that changes over time are easy to see, measured against the patient's own baseline and history. It does not diagnose, judge disease activity, or advise on treatment. Success is measured by how usable it is, whether people keep using it between visits, and whether clinicians find the summary useful.

Stage 2The clinic

A view built for clinicians and, over time, a connection to the electronic health record, so the summary is already in the chart when the visit begins. The appointment can then start from interpreting the record instead of rebuilding it from memory.

Stage 3Research

With patient consent and de-identification, these longitudinal records can support research: finding cohorts, designing trial endpoints that reflect how patients actually do over time, and identifying people who may be eligible for studies. The work starts in IBD, and the same approach can later extend to other chronic conditions that follow a similar course.

Team

A project of the Center for Therapeutics in Fibrosis and IBD.

The Center is an investigator-led effort focused on therapeutics and tools that reach patients. Perpetua is its patient-facing project, built by clinicians who treat IBD every week together with a software partner who builds for them.

Research study

A study of Perpetua is in preparation at Johns Hopkins.

Johns Hopkins investigators are preparing a study to evaluate Perpetua, a mobile app for people with long-term bowel conditions such as Crohn's disease and ulcerative colitis. With the app, you describe your symptoms by talking into your phone, and it assembles a short summary you can share with your doctor before a follow-up visit.

The study will look at whether the app is easy to use and whether it helps patients and clinicians talk more clearly about symptoms over time.

Enrollment is not yet open. This page will be updated when the study begins accepting participants. Nothing here is a solicitation to take part.
