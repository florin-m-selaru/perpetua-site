The problem

Patients lose the thread of their own disease between visits.

IBD has no cure and no straight line. Symptoms drift, flare and settle over weeks; medications change; there are scopes, labs, urgent-care trips, a course of steroids. Then, in a fifteen-minute appointment, a patient is asked to reconstruct months from memory and rank what mattered.

Daily diaries were meant to fix this and mostly fail: a diary is one more chore, a form flattens a lived experience, and there is little to show for it when the visit comes. In an early survey of people with IBD, most said forgetting details before seeing their doctor was a top frustration, and most relied on memory or a phone notes app between visits.

The most important details are the ones that get lost — and clinical decisions rest on what survives.

How it works

One record, three readings. The patient stays the author.

Capture

Tell it like you'd tell a friend. A voice note when something happens — a bad night, a missed dose, a new medication, a trip to urgent care. Perpetua asks one or two short follow-ups, in plain language, and never a 1-to-10 scale. Nothing to fill in on the days when nothing changed.

Your story

Entries become a timeline: symptoms in the context of treatment changes and clinical events, compared with your own baseline and your own past flares. Patterns you would only notice in hindsight become visible as they form.

The visit

Before an appointment, Perpetua drafts a concise summary for your clinician: what changed, when, how it compares with your usual, and what treatment or events matter for reading it. You review it, then share it yourself.

What Perpetua is not

Listens, clarifies, organises. Never authors, never advises.

  • It does not diagnose, estimate disease activity, or tell you whether you are in a flare.
  • It does not advise starting, stopping or changing any medication.
  • It does not contact your clinic or send anything on your behalf; you share your summary yourself.
  • It does not replace your care. If something is urgent, it tells you to call your clinic, or 911.
  • Your entries stay on your phone. Nothing about you is stored in the cloud.
  • It does not invent numbers you did not give.

The thesis

A faithful, patient-owned story is the connective tissue of care — and it compounds.

Symptoms only mean something relative to the individual: their baseline, their prior flares, the treatments they were on when things changed. A record built that way is worth more every month it exists — to the patient, then to the clinician, then to research. Perpetua is built in that order, and each stage has to earn the next.

Stage 1The prototypenow

An iPhone app for adults with established Crohn's disease or ulcerative colitis, built with Modern Logic and being evaluated with patients at Johns Hopkins. It answers one clinical question from the patient's own record: is this person at baseline, worsening, entering a possible flare, or changing after a treatment intervention? Success is measured in usability, sustained use between visits, and whether clinicians find the summary useful.

Stage 2The clinic

A clinician surface and, in time, integration with the electronic health record, so that the summary arrives where the visit happens and visits start at a higher level — interpreting, not reconstructing.

Stage 3Research

With consent, de-identification and patient ownership as the foundation, longitudinal stories become real-world evidence: cohort discovery, trial endpoints that reflect lived experience rather than a rigid questionnaire, and recruitment that finds the right people. IBD first; other chronic conditions that follow the same arc after.

Team

A project of the Center for Therapeutics in Fibrosis and IBD.

The Center is an investigator-led effort whose purpose is therapeutics and tools that reach patients. Perpetua is its patient-facing project, built by clinicians who treat IBD every week and a software partner who builds for them.

Principal Investigator

A gastroenterologist who treats inflammatory bowel disease and runs a laboratory devoted to its hardest complications. Perpetua began with his patients: disease that is highly individual, and a record that should learn each person's history rather than flatten it.
